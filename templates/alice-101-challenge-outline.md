# Alice §101 Challenge Outline
TrollProof Playbook – Troll Deterrence Kit

**Purpose:** Step-by-step outline to evaluate and attack weak software/AI patent claims under 35 U.S.C. § 101 (ineligible subject matter) following Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank (2014) and subsequent cases.
Use this to:
- Assess a troll's claim chart quickly
- Draft early motion to dismiss arguments
- Respond aggressively if they push back

**Key Rule (Alice Two-Step Framework):**
1. Step 1: Is the claim directed to a patent-ineligible concept? (e.g., abstract idea, law of nature, natural phenomenon)
2. Step 2: If yes → Does the claim include an "inventive concept" — something "significantly more" than the abstract idea itself?

Most broad AI/software claims die at Step 1 or fail Step 2.

**When this works best (2025–2030 trends):**
- Claims on "using AI to [do obvious thing]" (personalize, generate, analyze, recommend)
- No hardware tie-in or specific technical improvement
- Functional/result-based language ("a system configured to…")

**Quick Evaluation Checklist**

[ ] 1. Read the asserted independent claim(s) – focus on claim 1 (broadest)
   - Copy here: [paste claim text]

[ ] 2. Step 1 – Is it directed to an abstract idea?
   - Yes/No evidence:
     - Mental process? (can a human do it with pen/paper)
     - Fundamental economic practice? (pricing, matching, licensing)
     - Method of organizing human activity? (teaching, educating, social interaction)
     - Mathematical concept / algorithm? (AI training, optimization)
     - Generic computer implementation? (just "apply it on a computer")
   - Supporting cases:
     - Alice (financial hedging on computer)
     - Electric Power Group (data analysis/monitoring)
     - In re TLI (image classification on phone)
     - Recent 2024–2025: AI Visualize, Sanderling — displaying/processing data = abstract

[ ] 3. If Step 1 = abstract → Step 2: Inventive concept?
   - Does claim add "significantly more"?
     - Specific technical improvement? (e.g., new training method that reduces compute 50%)
     - Non-conventional arrangement? (ordered combination)
     - Not just "apply abstract idea on generic computer/AI model"
   - Red flags (almost always fail Step 2):
     - Claims use "processor", "memory", "AI model" as generic tools
     - "Configured to", "using AI to" language
     - No improvement to computer/AI itself

[ ] 4. Gather quick prior art / analogies (5–10 min)
   - Search Google Patents / arXiv for similar ideas published before filing date
   - Note: Your own defensive pubs or public posts count as prior art

[ ] 5. Draft Motion-to-Dismiss Arguments (template phrases)
   - "The asserted claims are directed to the abstract idea of [restate idea in one sentence, e.g., 'using AI to generate personalized content based on user input']."
   - "Under Alice Step 1, this is a mental process / method of organizing human activity implementable on generic hardware."
   - "Under Step 2, the claims add only well-understood, routine, conventional activity: applying the abstract idea using a generic processor, memory, and AI model."
   - "No inventive concept — no improvement to computer functionality, no unconventional arrangement."
   - Cite: Alice, Mayo, Electric Power Group, In re Killian (2023–2024 AI eligibility cases)

[ ] 6. Action Items
   - [ ] Send to troll in response letter (add to demand-response template)
   - [ ] If sued — file early 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss on §101
   - [ ] Document your analysis (dated note) — evidence of independent invention

**Quick Win Phrases for Letters / Filings**
- "The claims appear ineligible under §101 post-Alice. Please provide detailed Alice Step 1 & 2 analysis."
- "Absent a plausible inventive concept, we view the claims as directed to an abstract idea implemented on generic computing/AI technology."

**2025–2030 Watchlist**
- USPTO Guidance updates → AI-assisted inventions need significant human contribution
- Fed Cir trends → still hostile to broad "apply AI to X" claims
- If claim ties to specific hardware (e.g. edge device optimization) → harder to kill
